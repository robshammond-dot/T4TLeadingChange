Leading Change Through SCARF

Bringing the team with you through new systems, new processes and everyday change

🧠

Developed by Dr David Rock, SCARF names five things our brains treat as rewards or threats: Status, Certainty, Autonomy, Relatedness, Fairness. Any change touches at least one of them, and knowing which one helps you plan how to bring people along.

The Core Mindset
🎯
Resistance to change is usually a threat response, not a lack of willingness.

When a new system, a new process or a rota change meets pushback, the change itself is rarely the whole story. People are reacting to what it seems to mean for them: "Will I still be good at my job? What's expected of me now? Did anyone ask me?" Answer those questions early, and most of the resistance never needs to happen.

Our Values, As Leaders of Change
PASSION
Showing why you believe in a change is more convincing than announcing it.
PEOPLE
Knowing who will find a change hardest lets you support them before it starts.
PROFESSIONALISM
Clear timelines and consistent messaging protect Certainty and Fairness.
PRIDE
Recognising what people already do well protects their Status as things change.
PURPOSE
Involving people in how a change happens builds ownership of it.
SStatus Pride
🏅
A new system can make an experienced person feel like a beginner again overnight.

Someone who knew the old way inside out can suddenly feel slow or unsure in front of colleagues. That loss of confidence often shows up as criticism of the new tool, when what's really being protected is their sense of being good at the job.

👀Watch For
  • "The old way was quicker" from your most experienced people
  • Quietly working around a new system rather than asking for help
  • Reluctance to try something new in front of others
🛡️Protect It
  • Acknowledge the experience people bring, even as the tools change
  • Offer one-to-one help so nobody has to learn in public
  • Ask experienced team members to help shape how it's used
Try"You know our clients better than any system does. I'd value your eye on how we set this up."
CCertainty Professionalism
🧭
Change creates gaps in what people know, and people fill those gaps with worst-case guesses.

If the team doesn't know when a change starts, what it replaces, or how their work will be measured afterwards, they'll assume the worst. That worry shows up as questions, rumours and hesitation long before the change actually lands.

✅ Builds Certainty
  • Sharing a simple timeline: what changes, when, and what stays the same
  • Saying clearly what isn't decided yet, and when it will be
  • Giving everyone the same message, from both of you
  • Keeping the guides and training links in one place people know to look
❌ Erodes Certainty
  • Announcing a change with no start date or next step
  • Different answers depending on who is asked
  • Changing the plan midway without explaining why
  • Several changes landing at once with no sense of order
AAutonomy Purpose
🎛️
People support what they helped to shape.

A team that is simply told a process has changed will disengage faster than one that was consulted, even if the outcome is identical. You won't always be able to change the "what", but there is almost always room for input on the "how".

👀Watch For
  • "Why are we doing it this way now?" pushback
  • Compliance on paper, old habits in practice
  • Silence in meetings where input was invited
🛡️Protect It
  • Explain what's fixed and what's still open for input
  • Ask for ideas one-to-one as well as in the group
  • Show where the team's suggestions made it into the final version
Try"Here's the change and why. What would make this easier to work with day to day?"
RRelatedness People
🤝
People go through change more easily with someone they trust alongside them.

If a leader only appears when something new is being introduced, or when something has gone wrong, every announcement starts to feel like a threat. Regular, ordinary contact makes the bigger conversations easier.

⚠️

The groundwork happens before the change. Short, regular check-ins that aren't about performance or new processes build the trust that carries a team through the harder moments. They matter most when nothing is going on.

FFairness Professionalism
⚖️
A change that feels uneven will be resisted, even if it's small.

If one person has to adopt a new process and another quietly doesn't, or if the workload of a change lands on a few people, the change becomes about fairness rather than the work itself.

✅ Builds Fairness
  • The same expectations and timelines for everyone
  • Sharing the extra effort of a change across the team
  • Explaining the reasoning behind decisions like rotas and hours
❌ Erodes Fairness
  • Letting some people opt out of a new way of working
  • The same few people always absorbing the extra load
  • Decisions that seem to come from nowhere
Planning a Change: A Stage-by-Stage Check
Stage 1Before You Announce
  • Be clear on why the change is needed
  • Agree one message between the two of you
  • Think about who will find it hardest
  • Decide what's fixed and what's open for input
Stage 2Announcing It
  • Start with the "why", then the "what"
  • Share dates and what stays the same
  • Name what isn't decided yet
  • Invite questions, in the group and privately
Stage 3The First Few Weeks
  • Offer one-to-one support to anyone finding it hard
  • Expect things to be slower at first, and say so
  • Check in often, briefly
  • Recognise early progress out loud
Stage 4Settling In
  • Ask what's working and what isn't
  • Show which suggestions were acted on
  • Make sure everyone is on the new way
  • Retire the old way properly

Reading the Signal
🔎
When pushback feels bigger than the change, ask which S-C-A-R-F need is being touched.

A team member who criticises a new tool may really be worried about looking slow. One who questions a rota may really be asking whether it's fair. Responding to the real need usually settles things faster than defending the change itself.
